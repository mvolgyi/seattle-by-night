# Chapter 0 · What the Water Doesn't Wash Away

*Opening Fiction. Journal ID: @ID[CAP-00].*

## What the Water Doesn't Wash Away

By 2:40 a.m., Helen Ward had been listening to the same sentence for three hours.

It was spoken by a sixty-one-year-old woman in a kitchen in Lake City, with the TV on in the background and a dog that barked every time a car went by on the street. “My son didn't do drugs. My son was afraid of needles.” In the recording she said it twice, and both times her voice broke on the same syllable, the “fraid” in afraid. Helen had cut the first and kept the second. Then she'd put the first back and pulled the second, because the second sounded rehearsed and the first didn't, and on a podcast like hers people could smell rehearsed before the sentence was over.

Outside it was raining the way it rains in Ballard in November, half-hearted and endless. It was a fine rain that didn't soak you all at once but didn't let up either, and it left the whole neighborhood with the tired shine of a badly dried plate. The apartment was on the third floor, above a hardware store on Market Street that closed at six. It had two rooms and a window onto the fire escape, where Helen smoked even though the lease prohibited it. The walls of the room she called her studio were covered in printed maps of the city. There were red pins for the deaths, blue for the disappearances, and a few yellow ones she didn't know how to classify.

Nadia had sent her the mix of episode 113 at midnight, with an eleven-second voice memo: “It's good. Cut the dog. And get some sleep, Helen, seriously.” Helen hadn't cut the dog. She liked the dog. The dog was proof that the kitchen existed, that the woman wasn't an actress, and that the son had lived in a house with noise, with an animal waiting for him, with someone who knew he was afraid of needles.

He was the third in eighteen months. Three young men from Ballard and Crown Hill, dead of overdoses according to the county's report, no history, no old track marks, and all three with less blood than they should have had. That last part wasn't in any report. Helen knew because a tech at the medical examiner's office, who'd owed her a favor for years, had told her in a parking lot, looking the other way: “It's not that they were drained. They were short. Like somebody had taken a share.”

On the desk, plugged into the laptop by a cable fraying at the end, sat the drive. It was a black box, matte, the size of a paperback, with a strip of painter's tape on top where she'd written DO NOT TOUCH in marker. A second copy waited in a shoebox at the back of the closet, and a third lived in the cloud, in a folder with a deliberately boring name. Ten years of notes, audio, photos, clippings, screenshots of deleted threads, maps with dates. In her head she called it the basement, because that was where she went down when she didn't want to think about what was upstairs.

Inside the basement there was a folder with no name. Helen opened it rarely and always alone, with the door closed even though she lived alone. It held fourteen photos. The first was from 2014, from the fire at the Larchmont Building in Tacoma: a man with his back to the flames, in a dark jacket, looking toward a neighbor's camera as if he knew exactly where it was. The last was from 2025, from the docks on Harbor Island: the same man under a streetlight. Same jacket, or one just like it, and the same scar running from his right cheekbone down to the edge of his lip. It was the same face. Eleven years had passed and it hadn't gained a single line.

She was thirty-four and could already see the gray in the bathroom mirror.

The right-hand desk drawer held a flip phone, the kind nobody makes anymore, with a single number in its contacts. SAFE. No last name, no photo, no recognizable area code. That man had left it with her on a street by the port, the night she'd arrived at a scene too early and he was there, cleaning up, calm as someone sweeping his own kitchen. He'd said her name without her giving it. He'd quoted an article of hers that the paper pulled from its website six hours after it went up. And he'd told her something Helen remembered word for word, because she repeated it to herself when she couldn't sleep: “Keep looking. But when something's too big for you, you call this number first. Not the police. Not your podcast. Me.”

She'd never used it. She charged it every Sunday anyway, like someone changing the water in an empty vase.

At 2:50 she ran out of cigarettes.

She sat there a while staring at the empty pack, as if it might refill itself. The gas station on 15th Avenue was four blocks away and open all night. She pulled on her hoodie, shoved her keys and a few bills in the pocket, and left the laptop open with the Lake City woman's sentence paused, halfway through the word afraid.

Stepping into the hallway, she noticed the doorbell. In the spring the landlord had installed doorbell cameras on every door in the building, all the same brand, Gale Sentinel, free with a subscription the tenants paid for inside the rent without realizing it. Each one had a little blue light that stayed on all night, and Helen had gotten so used to it that she no longer saw it. That night she saw it because it wasn't there: the one on her door was off, and so was the one on 3B, across the hall. She filed it in the same place in her head where she filed the things that mean nothing until they mean something. *Lights out, 2:52.* And she went down.

The street was empty and shining. A 44 bus went by with its interior lights on and nobody in the seats. From the corner she could see, far off, the outline of the cranes along the canal, their little red lights blinking for planes that weren't coming. At the gas station, a kid in headphones rang her up without looking up. Helen paid cash, as always. Her father, who'd been a patrol officer in Tacoma for twenty-two years, used to say that everything you pay for with a card, somebody writes down, and the people who write things down aren't always who you'd think.

She got back at 3:04.

She knew something was wrong before she understood what. She climbed the stairs with the new pack in her hand, and the door of her apartment was closed, the way she'd left it, but the handle sat a little higher than it should. Helen always locked it with two turns, and with two turns the handle drooped. Tonight it was level. Someone had opened it and locked it again with just one.

And there was a smell in the hallway. Wet wool, first, like a coat that never dried right. Underneath, something metallic and sweetish, like the smell left on your hand after you've squeezed a fistful of coins for a long time.

Helen stood still on the top step. From inside came a sound she knew better than her own voice: the squeal of the studio closet door, which stuck halfway and had to be lifted a little to finish opening. Someone was lifting it. Nobody was talking. There were no hurried footsteps, no drawers dumped on the floor. It was the sound of someone working.

Her body decided before she did. She backed up the stairs, toward the landing by the roof door, slowly, flat against the wall, and crouched in the dark behind the railing, where the hallway light didn't reach. Her cell phone was in her pocket. She took it out and looked at it and put it back. She could call 911, and they'd ask what she was seeing, and she'd have to say she wasn't seeing anything, that there was a smell like coins and a door that squealed. And tomorrow there'd be a report with her name on it, and in a month the report would be gone, the way every report that touched what she touched disappeared.

She counted. She was at a hundred and eighty when the door opened.

There were two of them. Dark clothes with no logos, gloves, caps, and one carried a gray bag, the kind delivery drivers use. They moved the way people move when they've done it many times, without a word, without looking at each other. The one with the bag closed the door carefully, almost tenderly. The other stayed a second in the hallway, and Helen stopped breathing, because the man raised his head toward the stairs, toward the landing where she was, and breathed in through his nose. It wasn't the gesture of someone catching a strange smell. It was long and attentive, like a dog working a scent.

The one with the bag touched his shoulder. One touch, two fingers. *Time.* The two of them went down.

No engine started out on the street. What came was a low electric hum that moved off to the west and was lost in the rain.

Helen waited nine more minutes, counted, before she went down.

The apartment was almost untouched. That was the worst part. The TV was still there, so was the cash in the kitchen jar, and in the studio the laptop was still open with the Lake City woman's sentence on pause. The maps on the walls were whole, not a pin out of place. The frayed cable hung off the desk, plugged into nothing. The shoebox was gone from the closet. Helen sat down at the laptop, opened the cloud folder with the boring name, and the screen answered her in polite gray letters: *This folder is empty.* Last modified at 3:01 a.m.

They hadn't taken anything worth money. They'd taken the memory.

She sat on the floor a while, her back against the wall of maps. She thought about Danny Ruiz, who used to lend her his Media Law notes in college and laughed at her because she highlighted in three colors. Danny had lived on the fourth floor of the Larchmont Building in Tacoma until the night in 2014 when the building burned and seven tenants “could not be located.” There were no bodies. There was an article, hers, that the paper paid not to run. And there was a photo of a man with his back to the fire, looking at the camera.

She opened the right-hand drawer.

The flip phone had battery. Of course it did. She flipped it open, found the only entry in the contacts, and pressed the green button, and only realized her hand was shaking when the thing knocked against her ear.

It rang once. Then silence. Not the silence of a dead line: the silence of someone listening. In the background there was something that might have been rain on sheet metal, or the sea.

“Helen.”

It wasn't a question. The voice was deep and low, unhurried, as if he'd been waiting for her.

“They took the basement,” she said, and as soon as she said it she knew he couldn't know what the basement was, and she knew too that he wasn't going to ask.

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Are they still there?”

“No. There were two. They left ten minutes ago, in an electric car. They took the drive, the copy in the closet. And they wiped the cloud, at three-oh-one. They had my password or they didn't need it. One of them...” She stopped. “One of them was smelling the stairwell. Like a dog.”

There was a pause. It was short, but Helen had spent ten years listening to pauses for a living, and she recognized this one. It was the pause of someone who has just heard something he didn't want to hear.

“Don't touch anything,” he said. “Don't call anyone. Don't use the other phone.”

“How do you know I have another...?”

“Fifteen minutes.”

“Wait.” She pressed the phone against her face. “At least tell me who you are. Eleven years. I've got your face from 2014 and your face from last year, and it's the same. Tell me what you are.”

This time the silence was long. Helen heard the rain on her end and the rain, or the sea, on his.

“Someone who's usually late,” the voice said. “Not this time.”

And he hung up.

Helen closed the flip phone and sat with it in her hand. Then she got up, went to the door, and looked through the peephole. The hallway was empty and yellow. Across from her door, on the doorbell of 3B, the camera's little blue light had come back on. It shone steady, calm, looking straight at her.

She pulled up her hood, sat on the floor facing the door, and started counting.

Her father used to say that in this city the rain washes everything away. Helen had spent ten years proving it wasn't true, and someone had just sent her the bill.
